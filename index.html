LLM layer for a culturally responsive social robot

FACETlab, University of Pittsburgh · Prof. Erin Walker · Jan 2026 to present · NSF-funded

In a culturally responsive robotics summer camp, middle-school learners co-create robots and interact with the very robot they are building. A hybrid dialogue agent that combines an LLM with a rule-based framework helps them reflect on their work. I work on the LLM layer.

  • Designed few-shot and role-based prompting pipelines that keep the agent's dialogue tied to students' robot-building goals.
  • Designed personality-conditioned prompts that adapt the agent's traits to each student's preferred interaction style.
  • Previous studies by the lab found that students wanted more control over the robot's persona; during interactions, they wanted to change it or showed interest in it.
  • Implemented guardrails for age-appropriate interaction: system prompts, constraints, refusal policies and output filtering.
  • Made persona behavior testable by turning qualitative traits into quantitative constraints a program can check.
  • A/B tested prompt variants for coherence, safety, bias and educational effectiveness.
trait → rule → check
Trait or rule Written into the prompt How it is validated Type
curious persona ask a question when the current step calls for one count("?") against the step's condition soft · persona
unsafe request refuse requests outside age-appropriate limits refusal policy + output filter hard · guardrail

Hard rules never bend, but persona is a soft guardrail. The LLM follows 13 step instructions that guide the conversation, and each step has conditions, so whether the robot asks a question depends on what that step requires. This keeps the robot compliant with both its personality and its instructions without making the persona one-dimensional: a high-curiosity robot that asks questions all the time gets annoying, which students raised as an issue in an earlier study.

Teachable robot Emma: gaze and participation balance

FACETlab, University of Pittsburgh · Prof. Erin Walker · second study

Pairs of undergraduate students solve ratio word problems with a teachable robot, Emma. I analyze 13 recorded sessions to see how Emma's behavior shapes the way the two students take part.

  • Analyzed how the robot's gaze relates to learner interaction: whether students participate more when the robot looks at them, and how the type of interaction changes with its gaze.
  • Analyzed the dialogue qualitatively and measured the balance of participation between the two learners.
  • Ran statistical analyses that account for the influence of Emma's earlier actions on participation balance, to isolate the effect of Emma's most recent action on the dialogue that follows.

Paper in preparation: “Evaluating the Impact of a Robot's Multimodal Actions on the Participation Balance in a Human-Human-Robot Collaborative Learning Scenario” (co-author, data analysis).

Fairness in open-source AI-text detectors

Research project · CS 1684, University of Pittsburgh

Detectors that flag AI-written essays can misfire on writing by non-native English speakers. I measured that bias and reduced it while keeping AI detection accuracy at 90%.

  • Investigated bias in RoBERTa-based and DetectGPT detectors against non-native English writers.
  • Reduced false positives with domain-adaptive fine-tuning, multi-model ensembles and threshold calibration.
  • Measured bias with ROC/AUC, FPR/FNR analysis, chi-square tests and correlations with lexical diversity, syntactic complexity and sentence length.
How the results improved
  1. 1 · Base model

    87% of non-native essays wrongly flagged as AI (TOEFL essays)

  2. 2 · Fine-tuning, 3 attempts

    Fine-tuned the base model on three different mixes of native, non-native and AI-generated essays, then tested each one on the same essay sets.

    Training mixHuman correctAI caughtNon-native flagged
    (1) 33% native, 33% non-native, 33% AI77.8%81.8%22.9%
    (2) 50% non-native, 50% AI41.2%83.8%11.1%
    (3) 24% native, 76% non-native, plus AI31.1%91.6%25.3%

    (1) was the most consistent; (2) and (3) misclassified most general human essays.

  3. 3 · Ensembles, 2 attempts

    Combined models by averaging their output scores for each essay: first the base model with (1), then the base model with all three fine-tuned models.

    EnsembleHuman correctAI caughtNon-native flagged
    Base + (1)77.5%84.8%16.1%
    All 3 + base67.4%96.4%16.4%

    Adding (2) and (3) caught more AI essays but misclassified more human ones.

  4. 4 · Choose the best

    Chose the full ensemble (all 3 + base)

    It caught the most AI essays (96.4%) and was far less confident on its mistakes: 0.64 average confidence on human essays wrongly flagged, versus 0.97 for base + (1). Low-confidence mistakes can be separated with a threshold.

  5. 5 · Best + threshold (final)

    Human essays classified correctly: 67% → 83%, with 90% of AI essays still detected

    Non-native essays wrongly flagged: 87% (base model) → 9.5% on a separate learner corpus

    Decision threshold set at 0.65, just above the ensemble's average confidence on its mistakes.

  6. 6 · Comparison with DetectGPT
    MethodNon-native AUCNative AUC
    DetectGPT (zero-shot)0.9870.966
    Supervised classifier (RoBERTa-large)0.9971.000

    The classifier showed a slight bias against non-native essays, and DetectGPT a slight bias against native essays. Classifiers were more accurate and much faster; DetectGPT needs no training data.

Hybrid Quantum Model for Financial Prediction

Final project · CS 1613 Quantum Computation · Qiskit

A hybrid quantum-classical model that predicts whether a stock price will move up or down by evolving a quantum state through parameterized rotations and entanglement operations.

  • Designed a feature-encoding scheme in Qiskit that maps financial indicators such as volume and VIX into quantum states with rotation-Y (RY) gates.
  • Built entanglement-based dependencies between market factors to capture non-linear relationships.
  • Obtained the circuit parameters with classical optimization.

~70% average monthly accuracy on daily up/down predictions over 10 months (July 2025 to April 2026), with highs of ~85% in November 2025 and 87.7% in April 2026

USDT–ETH Solidity Trading Smart Contract

Solidity · Chainlink · Uniswap · MetaMask

A smart contract that automatically sells ETH for USDT when its price falls, to protect people and businesses that accept ETH payments from sharp losses.

  • Checks the Chainlink price feed on a time-based trigger (every 24 hours by default, configurable to hourly) and swaps ETH for USDT on Uniswap when the price falls 5% below the purchase price.
  • Protects each swap against sandwich attacks by setting its minimum output from the Chainlink price minus a slippage tolerance, with the Uniswap pool price used only as a sanity check.
  • Developed and tested with MetaMask on the Ethereum testnet and mainnet.
